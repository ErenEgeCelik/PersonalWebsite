I am a physics undergraduate at METU, and for the last year most of my
attention has gone into prediction markets — building the models, the data
infrastructure and the execution around them, then measuring whether any of
it actually works.

The pattern I keep coming back to is deriving before fitting. When I needed
a fair value for a binary contract on a Brownian price, the probit form
wasn't something I looked up; it is what the assumption produces. Every
parameter you fit instead of derive is a place a bad assumption can hide.
The second half of that habit is falsification: propose a model, then hunt
for the window where it must be wrong. What survives isn't proven — it is
merely not yet dead, which in physics and in markets is all you ever get.

The work here includes the results that made me look worse. A strategy I
retired because the measurement said to, and a model that was confidently
wrong in a way that looked exactly like an edge, are both written up in
full. If someone reads this and hires me on the strength of a backtest I
don't believe in, we are both going to find out eventually.
