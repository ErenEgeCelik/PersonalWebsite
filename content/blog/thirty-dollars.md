A paper account will let you be wrong forever at no cost. That is precisely the problem: nothing about it forces you to distinguish between a strategy that works and a strategy you like.

Thirty real dollars fixes this cheaply. The number is small enough that losing it teaches rather than hurts, and real enough that every sizing decision has a consequence you feel. When your entire capital is thirty dollars, a position that can lose a third of it is not a theoretical drawdown — it is next week's research budget.

The rule I set was simple: no additional deposits, ever. Whatever the account becomes, it becomes from its own P&L. That turns compounding from an abstraction into the only mechanism available, and it makes the ruin constraint bite at every single decision.

Three months later the account was around $1,200 in net P&L, still with no top-ups. The number is not impressive in absolute terms and I am not going to pretend it is. What it is, is honest: every dollar came from a decision I can point at and explain.
